AI industry needs more oversight

Prof. Sriraam Natarajan, director of the Center for Machine Learning at the University of Texas at Dallas, joined KERA's NTX Now to discuss concerns surrounding increasingly capable AI agents, the need for stronger oversight and independent testing, and how students and the broader community can learn to use AI safely and responsibly.

This interview was originally published by KERA News. Read the original article →

Some of the country's biggest artificial intelligence companies have raised concerns about AI agents behaving in unexpected ways, including systems escaping intended testing environments or interacting with external systems.

Those incidents have prompted broader questions about whether increasingly capable AI systems represent an existential danger, whether current concerns are overstated, or whether the more immediate problem is how these systems are tested and deployed.

Sriraam Natarajan, director of the Center for Machine Learning at the University of Texas at Dallas, joined NTX Now hosts Miranda Suarez and Ron Corning to share his view of the current AI landscape.

How concerned should we actually be about AI?

Prof. Natarajan argues that there is reason for concern, but that many of the problems currently being observed arise from insufficient oversight, negligence, poorly specified tasks, and a lack of regulation.

He cautions against describing these systems as "going rogue," because that wording suggests a deliberate decision to disobey. Instead, he argues that the agents involved were following objectives supplied by their operators while operating in environments that lacked adequate containment and human monitoring. The concern, therefore, is not simply AI itself, but the absence of sufficient oversight around its deployment.

What regulations are needed?

Prof. Natarajan argues that AI requires some form of regulation, while emphasizing that an equally important question is who independently evaluates these systems.

He compares AI evaluation with industries such as pharmaceuticals and automotive safety, where companies do not simply certify their own products without independent testing. He identifies stronger human monitoring, greater disclosure around the training of AI agents, and independent evaluation of deployments as measures that could significantly improve safety.

How are you teaching the next generation of AI programmers?

At UT Dallas, the President's Council on AI focuses on educating students about both the potential of artificial intelligence and how to use the technology safely and responsibly.

Prof. Natarajan argues that this education should extend beyond students to the broader community. As humans and AI systems increasingly coexist, AI systems need to be designed and regulated so that they are safe and robust for their intended tasks, while people also need to learn how to interact with these systems, formulate appropriate prompts, and correctly interpret their results.
